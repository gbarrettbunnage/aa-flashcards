Introduction

Audit risks



You are likely to be given information about the components in the group, the industries they are in and the related trading conditions as well as the group structure and controls over group financial reporting.



Therefore, you can expect audit risks to arise in three areas:



1

Risks within each component similar to an individual entity 



2

Risks arising from the group structure or changes in the group structure 



3

Deficiencies of controls that impact on the consolidation process 



You will need to consider the significance of risks within each individual component to assess whether they would be considered a significant RoMM at the group engagement level.



Internal control



You may be asked to describe tests of controls or evaluate control weaknesses in the group’s consolidation process. For example, a scenario could provide process notes detailing a client’s consolidation process and require you to evaluate any weaknesses identified and recommend appropriate control activities to improve the process. Understanding the types of controls in place and the reason for these controls will allow you to identify where weaknesses exist.



Substantive procedures



You may be required to produce a list of substantive tests over the consolidation for a group engagement in the assessment. You will have to identify the relevant specific substantive procedures that would be appropriate for this type of engagement.  



To do this, you should consider the relevant accounting policies, risks and assertions. You should approach the audit of the consolidation using the same methodology as for any other balance; consider the RoMM, accounting policies and assertions to ensure you have coverage of all areas requiring procedures.

Background on the group



&#x20;



Japanese Exports is a Scottish-based parent company with three wholly-owned subsidiaries: Hagane LLC (Hagane), Tama Steel Ltd (Tama) and Garden Blacksmith Ltd (Garden).  



The board of directors of Japanese Exports is a mixture of Scottish and Japanese nationals, some of whom are based in rural Japan, to demonstrate the multicultural nature of the operations and group.  



Japanese Exports was established in 1996 following a holiday to Japan where the founder, Justin Smith, was impressed by the quality of Japanese steel and, in particular, the garden shears and knives made in small family businesses.  



Hagane is an unlisted company in Japan that specialises in the manufacture of kitchen knives and garden equipment. Japanese blacksmiths use a traditional and unique manufacturing process that goes back generations and the manufactured Japanese steel is world famous for its quality, strength and longevity. As a result, products made with Japanese steel attract premium selling prices.  



Tama imports goods manufactured by Hagane and sells to the UK market. A number of goods have attracted a cult following in horticultural and culinary arenas and are known as a ‘product for life’. 



Garden is a registered charity established in conjunction with a major horticultural training school in Japan. It facilitates the exchange of students from Scotland and Japan to learn the skills and cultures of both countries. 



The previous auditors of Japanese Exports resigned suddenly part way through the current financial year and Fogel is being considered as the new auditors. One of Fogel’s senior audit partners heard a rumour that the reason for the resignation was an inability to access information from Hagane and its external auditors in a timely manner, which resulted in significant delays in issuing the group audit report. 



There has been an allegation of inappropriate use of funds, by a junior employee within Garden, during a recent 6-month exchange of students between Scotland and Japan. This has resulted in adverse press coverage largely due to one of the charity trustees being a prominent member of the UK government. The trustees of Garden have taken a very proactive stance to these allegations and have appointed an independent law firm to investigate. All individuals involved in the allegations are currently on paid leave at home. 



Recently, Japanese Exports renegotiated their financing facility with their UK-based bank. This followed a breach of the group’s overdraft facility, which has been utilised near its limit for a number of years. At this stage, no further details of the facility have been made available to Fogel.  



There are no native Japanese speakers in the current audit engagement team for the group audit, although, within the wider Fogel office, there is one qualified CA who is both an English and Japanese speaker.

(Note: The risk of using a component auditor is not to be considered since the audit partner has stated that it does not need to be covered).





Solution



Select each of the identified risks to review the evaluation.





Resignation of previous auditors

Japanese Exports’ previous auditor resigned part way through the year.



Evaluation of risk:

It will be necessary to contact the previous auditors to determine their reasons for resigning. Although we are aware of a rumour around the resignation, there may be further reasons which could impact our decision to accept the engagement.



Depending on the reasons for resignation, we may not wish to accept the engagement.





Overseas locations

The audit is fairly complex with overseas locations and subsidiaries involved.







Evaluation of risk:

There is a risk that we may not have sufficient resources to complete the audit or gather evidence from the various subsidiaries and locations to inform the audit opinion.





Charity status

Garden is a registered charity that is required to comply with various laws and regulations relating to charities.  







Evaluation of risk:

The statutes and regulations relevant to Garden will require to be applied. There is a risk that we do not have the relevant expertise within the audit team to audit such a subsidiary.



There may be suspicion of inappropriate use of funds. In addition to a potential increased fraud risk, this can give rise to reputational risk by being associated with this potential client.





Japanese subsidiary

Hagane is located in Japan. There will be various Japanese laws and regulations that impact on the operations of this company.  







Evaluation of risk:

There is the risk that we do not have sufficient knowledge and understanding or experience of these Japanese regulations. The language barrier may also be an issue.





Board of directors

The board are not all based in the UK and several directors are located in rural Japan.  







Evaluation of risk:

We will require to undertake client identification procedures for all directors. This may be problematic given the location of some of the directors in rural Japan.







Remote monitoring of the company may also lead to a weak control environment.





Breach of covenant and refinancing

The financing facility has been renegotiated following a breach of the group’s overdraft facility, which has been utilised near its limit for a number of years.







Evaluation of risk:

This may have an impact on going concern, making the audit of this potential client riskier. This is because the assumptions used in going concern assessment require judgement and therefore more scrutiny. There is also a reputation risk if we are associated with a failing business.



This also gives rise to a possible incentive to commit fraudulent financial reporting, increasing the risk related to accepting this client.

APPENDIX 2 – Notes of a meeting held with James MacDonald  



&#x20;



Business structure and operations 



EAST purchases high-value antiques and replicas using a network of buyers based in the Far East, predominantly Japan. The buyers are subcontracted antiquities specialists who purchase on EAST’s behalf to satisfy the requirements of specific customer orders. 



EAST’s main office is based in London, where the administration and finance staff operate. The administration staff, based in the UK office, deal with enquiries and confirming orders from customers and provide instructions for buyers in the Far East. The finance staff are responsible for raising and processing sales invoices, paying all salaries and processing cash receipts from customers. 



To deal with language differences, the company also has a small office in Tokyo, which is staffed by one local administrator, who is responsible for liaising with the buyers, instructing them on the items that are required and fielding any queries that they might have. The administrator deals with invoices sent in from suppliers for goods purchased and from buyers charging their commission on specific orders, and it has the authority to review and make payment from the company’s Japanese yen account. 





The Tokyo administrator makes payment on all invoices within 14 days of receiving the documentation. The purchase invoices are then scanned and sent to the London office, where they are posted onto EAST’s purchase ledger.



EAST – Business performance for the year



Revenue and profit before income taxes for the 6 months to 31 January 20X3 were in line with budget, but during the second half of the financial year, the company has experienced a significant drop in the number of enquiries and hence revenue. Due to the difficult economic conditions and declining returns on fine arts and antiques, many of the company’s long-established customers are no longer investing their money in these types of assets. Thus, EAST did not achieve the profit levels it budgeted for and made a loss of £350,000 for the year ended 31 July 20X3.  



&#x20;



Revenue  



On receipt of an enquiry from a customer, the London staff will email the administrator in Tokyo with the details of the goods sought, who will then liaise with the buyers who will try and source the goods. Based on their knowledge of previous auctions and supplier contacts, the buyers carry out the necessary research and then email the administrator in Tokyo with an estimated price for the goods, the buyer’s commission for the order, and a separate estimate for delivery costs. This is forwarded to the London office, where a quote is prepared for the customer, including the mark-up that will be charged by EAST. The quote is emailed to the customer who agrees or disagrees, via email or letter.  



Once the order has been confirmed and the goods have been sourced, the buyer will arrange shipping and delivery of the goods, on behalf of EAST, directly to the customer. As the delivery contracts and insurance policies are undertaken by EAST, the significant risks and rewards of ownership are retained by EAST until the goods have been delivered to the customer. Once the buyer confirms shipment of the goods, the administrator in Tokyo instructs the London office to raise the invoice to the customer. The revenue is recognised as soon as the invoice is raised, and sometimes the invoice reaches the customer before the goods even arrive in the UK. The company requests payment within 14 days of receipt of the invoice. 



&#x20;



Cash and bank  



EAST operates three bank accounts. Its two current accounts are held with the Bank of Lotus Tokyo, of which one is a Japanese yen account and the other a sterling account. The sterling account, maintained in the UK, is used to receive customer receipts and pay UK salaries and expenses. The Japanese yen account, operated predominantly by the administrator in Tokyo, is used to pay suppliers and the buyers in Japan. James transfers funds into the yen account when necessary to cover these payments. 



EAST has a short-term deposit account at London Metro Bank. James stated that, as he alone has access to the bank balances via the internet, he regularly withdraws money from the current accounts to place it on short-term deposit. As part of the deposit process, the money is transferred back into the main bank accounts after the term has ended. He is responsible for managing these transactions and has asked that the audit team speak to him directly for any information required on the cash balances. He performs bank reconciliations on a quarterly basis.  



James explained that the increase in cash on deposit at the year end was due to a customer paying in advance for goods. A payment of £500,000 was received early in July from a new customer whose goods have not been sourced yet. The receipt has been placed on short-term deposit with other excess funds and a deferred income account set up in the nominal ledger. James explained that this is the first time that this has occurred, and he has not yet contacted the customer to understand the reasons for this advance payment. 



&#x20;



Provisions 



In May, legal proceedings were initiated against EAST by a well-known and respected customer regarding a dispute over the authenticity of antiques that had been sold to him by EAST. The customer has argued that the products they received were counterfeit. 



A provision for £250,000 has been set up to cover refunding the cost of goods sold, legal fees and other potential costs that may be payable due to the legal claim that has been made against EAST.  



James is certain that there is no truth to the allegations but feels it is necessary to provide for the costs as management intends to fight the case in court. He considers it fundamental to EAST’s business reputation that these allegations are found to be false. 



&#x20;



Foreign currency 



EAST purchases goods in local currency in the country of origin (normally Japanese yen). EAST bills its customers in sterling as the customers are all based in the UK. To record the purchase transactions on the goods the buyers have sourced in its creditors ledger, EAST has a policy of setting an exchange rate for a 6-month period, which James explained was to make it easier for staff to record supplier invoices, as there have been significant fluctuations in the exchange rate.

A key point to keep in mind is that as acceptance procedures have been completed, acceptance risks do no need to be considered.

Substantive procedures 



Bank – completeness, existence, accuracy, valuation and allocation 



There are large cash transactions around the year end – there are several deposits and withdrawals affecting EAST’s current accounts that do not all appear to be recorded in the general ledger.  



There is also an increased risk of fraud in the cash balance with the frequent and unaccounted movements in the cash balance and only one person overseeing it.



&#x20;



Payments are made from the Japanese yen account before the invoices are recorded on the ledger, which could result in transactions not being recorded accurately.  



&#x20;



There is also an unreconciled difference in the year-end bank reconciliation of the current accounts that James says he is investigating.  



&#x20;



These issues indicate a risk that there are several unrecorded transactions and therefore the bank balance is not complete, is overstated or is valued incorrectly. 



The risk of over statement is significant as the increase in cash balance over budget is almost entirely what is keeping the company in a net asset position.



Inspect a sample of deposits, transfers and withdrawals included in the bank statements and trace these to verify their inclusion within the general ledger. 



Enquire about the unreconciled difference on the yen bank account reconciliation and agree to supporting documentation, and agree reconciling amounts to journal entries to ensure necessary adjustments have been posted for payments made where invoices are not yet raised.



Recalculate year-end bank reconciliations for all accounts, agreeing to the bank statements and general ledger, and agree reconciling items to post-year-end bank statements or supporting documentation.



Obtain a bank letter directly from each bank holding accounts to confirm the balances, terms of the accounts and any restrictions.



For the short-term deposit account, inspect the terms and conditions to verify that this has been correctly classified as cash and cash equivalents.

Revenue recognition – cut off and occurrence 



The company currently recognises revenue as soon as customer invoices are raised rather than when goods are delivered. 



&#x20;



&#x20;



As such, the company is recognising income too early and there is a risk that revenue is overstated at the year end.  



Inspect a sample of shipping notes pre- and post-year end, along with evidence of customer receipt of the goods, and agree to when the sales invoice is posted in the ledgers to ensure revenue is recognised in the correct period.



Payables/expenses – completeness 



Administrator in Tokyo can review and make payment of invoices sent in by suppliers/buyers and there is a time lag before these are recorded on the ledger. The bank account also does not reconcile due to probable unrecorded transactions.  



&#x20;



There is a risk that these transactions are not recorded within the accounts and therefore that expenses and liabilities could be incomplete.



Inspect a sample of payments included within the ledger for the yen account and verify that the expense and liability have been included within the appropriate ledgers.



For a sample of supplier/buyer invoices received post-year end, inspect invoice and ledger to agree that a liability is recorded at the year end where the invoice is in relation to a pre-year-end transaction.



Provision for legal costs – rights and obligations/existence/completeness 



This is a one-off provision for £250,000 relating to an ongoing legal dispute. The provision includes the cost of refunding the cost of goods, as well as estimated legal fees and other potential costs.



&#x20;



There is a risk that these costs are not accurate or complete and therefore the provision could be over or understated.



Recalculate provision, agreeing the amounts to underlying documentation to support the legal and other costs (eg solicitors’ correspondence, fee notes etc).  



Obtain direct confirmation from solicitors of the estimated value of legal costs and potential damages and the likelihood of damages being paid. 



If the claim has been settled post-year end, inspect settlement documentation and consider the impact this has on the level of the year-end provision.



Foreign exchange – accuracy, valuation and allocation 



EAST sets its exchange rates for a 6-month period rather than translating at the rate at the date of the transaction.  



&#x20;



Due to the large fluctuations in the currency rates, this could result in significant differences in the value at which transactions and balances are recorded, and the difference on foreign exchange may not be correctly recorded in the statement of profit or loss.  



Select a sample of transfers from the sterling bank account to the yen account/sample of foreign currency purchases from throughout the year, and  recalculate the value of the transfer/transaction based on the exchange rate at the date of the transaction. Compare this to the value of the transaction recorded in the general ledger for accuracy.  



Alternatively, this can be done for all transactions instead of a sample using data analytics. 



Weak control environment 



Quarterly instead of monthly bank reconciliations, evidence of a lack of authorisation and review of transactions, specifically in relation to bank transfers and a distinct lack of segregation of duties within the Tokyo office, are indicative of a weak control environment within the company. These factors could result in errors occurring within the financial statements and increase the risk of fraud.  



Select transactions susceptible to management override (payments to buyers, bank transfers) and agree these to supporting documentation, ensuring the supporting documentation is original, not scanned or emailed copies. Consider requesting confirmation of the transactions from the third parties to support the validity of the entries.

Going concern
