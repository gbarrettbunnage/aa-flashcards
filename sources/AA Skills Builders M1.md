#### Question 1



Read the following scenario.

You are an audit manager on the audit of ABC limited (ABC), a company specialising in the manufacture and supply of sporting equipment. ABC has been an audit client for 10 years and you have been audit manager for the past 3 years, while the audit partner has remained unchanged. You are now planning the audit for the year ending 31 December 20X4.



Your firm has been asked by ABC’s directors to prepare the company’s financial statements as its accounting department is understaffed this year and is being led by a part-qualified assistant accountant.



As the company’s auditors, ABC’s directors would like you and the audit partner to attend an upcoming evening reception in a hotel, where ABC will present their future expansion plan to banks and existing major shareholders.  



ABC has indicated that the fee for last year’s audit will be paid as soon as their cash flows improve. 



Finally, an audit senior on the audit has just inherited about 3% of ABC’s share capital as an inheritance on the death of a distant relative.



Question: 



Evaluate the ABC engagement, identifying any ethical threats, including the potential implications, and recommending appropriate safeguards to mitigate each threat.

Ethical issue 1. Rotation of audit partner

ABC has had the same audit partner for the last 10 years. An audit partner’s independence and judgement may be impaired due to a familiarity threat, and also self-interest and self-review threats.



The long association may cause the auditor to become too familiar and too trusting with the client, resulting in less professional scepticism being exercised and the possibility of material misstatements remaining undetected.



This, in turn, gives rise to a self-interest threat in that the long association and close relationship with the client create a personal interest which may inappropriately influence the auditor’s professional judgement or behaviour.



A self-review threat may also arise as the partner might be reluctant to identify any errors or misstatements in relation to the audit of prior years’ financial statements.



According to the FRC’s ES, rotation of an audit partner of a non-public interest client such as ABC should be considered after 10 years in the role.



If this cannot be carried out, the reasons should be documented, ABC’s those charged with governance (TCWG) should be informed and alternative safeguards should be put in place. 



The audit partner should be rotated.



If this is not possible, safeguards need to be applied, such as applying independent quality reviews to the engagement.





Ethical issue 2. Preparation of financial statements

Preparing financial statements as well as auditing them would give rise to a self-review threat. The auditor may not notice any errors, or want to report errors that they have previously prepared.



&#x20;



This will also lead to the firm undertaking a management role as they will be involved in exercising judgement regarding accounting estimates.



&#x20;



According to the FRC’s ES, audit firms must not provide any service to private clients if a part of the service involves the audit firm undertaking a management role.  



&#x20;



It is likely that the firm will end up taking up some management responsibility as the department is currently being led by a part-qualified assistant accountant.



&#x20;



This assignment should therefore not be accepted.





Ethical issue 3. Attendance at a social event

Attending the social event may be interpreted as supporting ABC in these expansion plans. There is an advocacy threat to independence. An advocacy threat arises when the auditor is asked to promote or represent their client in some way.



Support for a client may imply that the audit firm are ‘too close’ to that client and may therefore lose their independent view regarding the audit. There is also a familiarity threat. 



The firm should therefore decline the dinner invitation, stating their reasons. 





Ethical issue 4. Unpaid audit fee

The unpaid fee in respect of last year’s audit could be construed as a loan to the audit client.



A self-interest threat to independence arises as the closure of the loan may be interpreted as more important than providing an appropriate audit opinion.



The firm’s ethics partner should be notified of the overdue fee. 



It needs to be considered if the audit firm can continue or whether it is necessary to resign as the fee is not clearly trivial. 



If the firm does not resign, a review of the audit work by a partner who is not involved in the engagement should be conducted.





Ethical issue 5. Inheritance

The inheritance of shares is a direct financial interest in an audit client. 



This will give rise to a self-interest threat to independence as the senior’s judgement may be compromised.



An audit team member is not allowed to have a direct financial interest in the client.



Therefore, they need to be removed from the audit team. 



#### Question 2



Read the following scenario. 



&#x20;



Tom has been the engagement partner on the audit of DEF limited (DEF) for 3 years. He regularly plays golf with the finance director at DEF. During a recent golf match, the finance director asked if the legal arm of the audit firm would be willing to represent DEF in court, to defend an unfair dismissal claim that has been brought by an ex-employee. If the claim is successful, the amount owed would be material in relation to the financial statements of DEF. 



The finance director’s daughter has recently joined the firm as an audit trainee and has asked if she could join the audit team for DEF as she wants to help her father in his business.

Evaluate the DEF engagement, identifying any ethical threats, including the potential implications, and recommending appropriate safeguards to mitigate each threat.

Ethical issue 1: Close relationship

The audit engagement partner and finance director regularly play golf together. The fact that the finance director and audit engagement partner ‘regularly’ play golf together suggests a close relationship has been formed.



A close relationship would create a familiarity threat as the audit engagement partner may be too trusting of the finance director, or too sympathetic to the finance director’s interests. 



If the engagement partner no longer appears to be independent of the client, he should be removed from the audit team and rotated.



Alternatively, an independent partner review may be appropriate in order to ensure the engagement partner has performed his work objectively. 





Ethical issue 2: Litigation support service

The finance director has asked the firm to represent DEF in court to defend an unfair dismissal claim. Representing an audit client in the resolution of a dispute in court, where the amounts are material to the financial statements, would create a significant advocacy and self-review threat. 



The auditor would be, and be perceived to be, promoting the client’s interests and, therefore, not independent.



In addition, if an auditor was to represent their client, they would not be able to objectively review the provision included in the financial statements in relation to the case.



The threat would be so significant that no safeguard could reduce the threat to an acceptable level.



Therefore, the auditor should politely refuse the request, explaining the reasons for their decision.





Ethical issue 3: Family relationship

The finance director’s daughter has requested to join the audit team. This would create a familiarity threat, as well as potential self-interest and intimidation threats.



The immediate family relationship creates a familiarity threat as the finance director’s daughter would not be able to review the financial statements objectively as she would be too trusting of their work and too sympathetic to their interests. 



A self-interest threat (or intimidation threat) may be created as she may conceal material misstatements she identifies during the audit to protect her father’s interest.



She should not work on the audit or any other assignments that the audit firm undertakes for DEF.





Ethical issue 4: Fee dependence

14% of the firm’s total fees are from services provided to DEF.  



The high level of recurring fees earned from them creates a self-interest threat. Because of their dependence on the client, the auditor may be more concerned about retaining the client (and engagements) and protecting the fees than forming the correct opinion on the financial statements.



The recurring fees from the client should be regularly monitored to ensure they do not breach the FRC’s fee limits (15% for non-public interest clients). 



The level of fees earned is approaching the limit and therefore should be discussed with the firm’s ethics partner and client’s those charged with governance. 



An independent partner review may be appropriate in order to ensure the audit team has performed their work objectively.
